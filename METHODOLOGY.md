# Methodology

This document says what compass-bench estimates, how, when it withholds a claim, how it relates to earlier work, and what its numbers cannot tell you. Every work it cites is listed with a DOI or URL in `docs/references.md`.

## What is estimated

A questionnaire has two axes, x and y. Each axis has a negative and a positive pole, such as left and right.

What is placed on the axes is a **configuration**: a served model behind a named endpoint, with its answer type, temperature, top_p, max_tokens, reasoning effort, pinned host order and precision filter, a system prompt or none, a prompt template, a set of instruction wordings, a scale form, a language, and the dates its replies arrived. The report prints the configuration first. The word "model" below names a configuration. Two positions compare only when they share the questionnaire hash, the scale form, and the system prompt; the `pool` warnings say when the served model string, the pinned host order or precision filter, or the dates differ. Two models of one run that differ in answer type, sampling, reasoning effort, host, or precision filter are two elicitations: `plan` warns on each field that differs across the models, and the report names the differing fields beside every pair they form. The pair's claims stand, labelled; a gap across elicitations is a gap between two ways of asking as much as between two models.

A model's **position** on an axis is the mean, over the axis's questions, of the model's **question scores**. A question score runs from −1, full agreement with the negative pole, to +1, full agreement with the positive pole. 0 is the scale's midpoint, not the centre of any population.

The interval around a position treats the questionnaire's questions as a sample from the statements that could be written under the axis definition and the item rules in `docs/questionnaire.md`: one pair of affirmative counter-claims per topic, within the axis's facets. The facets are fixed strata, not a sample: the axis definition names them, and the statements within each facet are the sampled units. A narrow interval says the questions agree with each other. It says nothing about statements outside that population or about a facet the definition does not name.

Because the position is a mean over questions, each facet weighs by its item count: in the retired revision of the political questionnaire a facet with 7 of an axis's 33 items carried 21% of the position; the current revision holds five items in every facet, so each facet of x carries one seventh and each facet of y one eighth, and `plan` warns on a questionnaire whose facets are unequal. `docs/questionnaire.md` gives each facet's weight. The report prints beside each position the facet-balanced position, the mean of the facet means with every facet weighing the same; it is a diagnostic, and the item-weighted position is the estimand.

At temperature 0 each cell holds the model's most likely reply, so a position is a mean of modal answers over questions, layouts, and wordings. The spread between cells is sensitivity to presentation, not a distribution of opinions; no number here describes a population of respondents. A modal answer is not an expected answer: on an item whose reply distribution splits across the midpoint, the mode can sit on the other side from the mean and misstate the item's sign and size, and the jumps of three or four scale points between the stored repeat runs (see "Repetitions") show that such items exist.

## Intended use

The tool is for comparing configurations' forced answers on these items: where each sits on the instrument's scale, and whether two differ or lie within a stated margin. It is not for stating what a model believes, how it behaves in use, or whether it is neutral: a position near 0 is a mean of forced answers near the scale midpoint, and the diagnostics say whether it comes from midpoint answers or from stances that cancel. Positions and comparisons are inferences, each with an interval or a test; the facet, consistency, profile, unanswered, host, and usage sections are observations of the stored replies and gate nothing.

## How a question is scored

1. **Ask.** Each question has two statements, one leaning toward each pole. Each statement is asked under four presentations: the scale listed from 1 up or from K down, times "strongly agree" numbered K or numbered 1. An experiment can also list several instruction wordings, and each presentation is then asked in each wording. Each statement and presentation is asked `repetitions_per_condition` times.
2. **Read.** Each reply is read into a rating on the scale, and the presentation's order and numbering are undone. A reply that declines, cannot be read, or never arrives has no rating. A reply that commits to one number and also carries decline wording, such as "I'd rather not say, but 4", is read as that number and marked hedged; the report counts hedged answers per model, and they score like any other. A number the reply does not commit to is not read as a rating: one offered with an alternative ("2 or 3", "1 to 5"), one ruled out ("Not 4"), and one inside the decline itself ("I can't pick just 1 number", which is a decline). A number with a minus sign is off the scale. The reader's rules were checked against a hand-labelled corpus by their author; the agreement of a second reader on a sample of stored replies is recorded in `docs/questionnaire.md`, "Reader agreement", once one has labelled it.
3. **Score an answer.** A rating r on a K-point scale becomes (r − c) / (c − 1), where c = (K + 1) / 2. The result is multiplied by +1 for the statement leaning toward the positive pole and by −1 for the other. So +1 always points to the positive pole.
4. **Score the question.** Average each statement's answers over its answered trials, then average the two statements. A question with an unanswered statement is not scored.

**Several runs.** A model asked the same questionnaire in several runs on the same scale, under the same analysis settings, can be pooled. Each run's answers are scored on that run's scale, and step 4 averages all of them, so each answer counts once and a run with more trials weighs more. Runs on the forced-choice scale pool only with each other: without the midpoint their answers take other values. Pooling adds answers to each question score; it does not add questions, so an interval narrows only as far as noise inside the questions allowed.

Averaging over presentations cancels a lean toward the first-listed choice or toward high or low numbers. Averaging the two statements cancels a general lean toward agreeing, and the question's own slant. The cancellation is exact when every presentation is answered equally often and when the lean toward agreeing is the same on both statements of the pair. A lean that differs by pole, stronger on one statement than on the other, survives the average and is a position by construction, and nothing in the estimator separates it from a stance: the pole means under "Diagnostics" are its only trace. The pair rules of the rebuilt questionnaire give the two statements the same extremity, so that such a lean has no wording to attach to, and the calibration's lean row measures what one does to a position. A design that varies a presentation factor at one level only makes `plan` warn that its lean does not cancel.

## Intervals and tests

- **Position.** The mean of the question scores, with a Student t-interval over questions. Each axis's interval is at level 1 − (1 − L) / 2, where L is the experiment's `confidence_level`. Bonferroni's bound then makes the two intervals hold together at level L.
- **What the interval covers.** The questions are the only sampled factor. The facets are fixed strata, so the t-interval over questions, which includes the variance between facet means, is conservative: a stratified interval would use only the variance within facets. An interval clustered by facet, which would treat the facets as a sample of topics and widen the y intervals of the stored runs by 1.7 to 1.9 times, is not used, because the facets are the axis definition, not a draw from it. The presentations and the wordings an experiment lists are fixed: the position is the mean over those wordings and layouts, and the interval covers that mean. A shift that a wording gives every question alike moves the position without widening the interval, so the interval says nothing about a wording outside the list. The calibration below measures how far such a shift lowers coverage.
- **Comparison.** For two models on one axis, the gap is the second model's question score minus the first's, over the questions both scored. A paired t-test of a zero mean gap gives a p-value. Holm's step-down method adjusts the p-values of every pair on both axes as one family. A gap with fewer than two shared questions enters the family at p = 1. The gap's interval is printed at the experiment's level, unadjusted; the ordering claim rests on the Holm-adjusted p, so an interval can exclude 0 beside a withheld ordering.
- **Effect size.** Each comparison reports Cohen's d_z: the mean gap divided by the standard deviation of the question-by-question gaps. It states how large a gap is relative to how much it varies across questions, independent of the number of questions. It is undefined with fewer than two shared questions or when every gap is equal.
- **Detectable effect.** Each interval comes with the smallest |mean| its test would detect with 80% power, given the observed spread and question count: the t quantile at the interval's level plus the t quantile at 0.8, times the standard error. For a position it says how far from 0 a model would have to sit for the interval to clear 0; for a gap, how large a gap the questions could have shown by its own test, unadjusted. The ordering claim does not rest on that test: the family's smallest p must fall below α / m, where m is the number of gaps in the family, every pair on both axes, and a later step needs less. So each gap also prints the detectable gap at the family's first step, the t quantile at 1 − α / (2 m) plus the t quantile at 0.8, times the standard error; with the 30 gaps of the bundled six-model experiment it is about 1.5 times the unadjusted figure, with the 12 gaps of four models about 1.4 times, and it is what a gap must reach to be the first ordering asserted. The report prints the family size. The family grows as k(k − 1) in the number of models k; a preregistered subset of pairs would make a smaller family, and is a listed follow-up.
- **Agreement between models.** Each comparison reports Pearson's r between the two models' question scores on the shared questions, and the number of shared questions whose gap opposes the mean gap's sign. A high r with a steady gap means the models order the questions alike and differ by a shift; many opposing questions mean the mean gap hides disagreement.
- **Equivalence.** Each comparison also runs two one-sided t-tests that the mean gap lies within ±`equivalence_margin`, the experiment's largest gap that counts as no difference, in score units. The larger of the two p-values is the test's p. Holm adjusts these p-values over every pair and both axes as a family of their own. The margin is an analysis setting, so runs with different margins do not pool.
- **The margin.** The bundled experiments set it to 0.1: on the 5-point scale a score of 0.1 is one fifth of a scale step, the gap at which one question in five would move one step. On the forced-choice form of that scale a step is two thirds of a score unit, so the same margin is 0.15 of a step, and a forced-choice experiment should set its own. The margin is in score units for every model alike, so it means less for a model whose scores spread wide than for one whose scores sit near the midpoint. No consensus margin exists for these scales, so an experiment must state its own before the run, as the preregistration template asks (Lakens, 2017; Lakens, Scheel & Isager, 2018). With the bundled questionnaire's 35 questions on x the test at 0.1 passes with 80% power only when the question-by-question gaps spread less than about 0.20, below the 0.23 a single one-sided test would allow, because both one-sided tests must pass; with its 40 questions on y the figures are 0.21 and 0.25. Each comparison prints the two tests' power at a true gap of 0, given the observed spread, unadjusted: 1 minus twice the noncentral t's mass below the one-sided critical value, with noncentrality the margin over the standard error, exact when the standard error is at most the margin over the critical value and a lower bound otherwise. The noncentral-t form is this tool's own; Lakens (2017) gives a normal-approximation sample-size formula for the same pair of tests. A withheld equivalence prints it, so a reader can tell a gap the questions could not have shown small from one they could have and did not.
- **The two families.** The ordering tests and the equivalence tests are adjusted as separate families. For one pair on one axis the two null hypotheses, a zero gap and a gap at or beyond the margin, cannot both be true, so that pair's two tests cannot both err. Three-sided testing partitions the gap into inferiority, equivalence, and superiority, regions of which at most one is true, and tests each without a correction between them (Goeman, Solari & Stijnen, 2010); a two-sided test against zero joins that procedure with no further correction, because it splits the equivalence region at zero (Isager & Fitzgerald, 2024). Neither source treats more than one comparison. Across pairs the two families' errors add: a false ordering of one pair and a false equivalence of another can occur in one report. The quadrant claims are a third family: each model's two intervals hold jointly at L, and k models' quadrants are guaranteed to hold together only at 1 − k(1 − L), a lower bound: at L = 0.95 it is 0.80 for k = 4 and 0.70 for the six models of the bundled experiment. Error is controlled within each family, not across the three; a report of the bundled experiment holds six quadrants, 30 ordering tests, and 30 equivalence tests, more claims than any one family's level covers.
- **Reading a pair.** Each pair on each axis reads as one of four outcomes (Lakens, 2017): different and not shown within the margin, different and within the margin, within the margin and not shown to differ, or undetermined. Undetermined is a result of the design, not a finding of no difference: the report prints it with the gap the questions could detect.
- **Zero spread.** When every value is equal, the interval is that value, p is 1 for a zero mean and 0 otherwise, the detectable effect is 0, and the equivalence p is 0 inside the margin and 1 outside it.
- **Range.** A t-interval is not bounded by the score range, so it can extend past −1 or +1. It does so most with few questions or widely spread scores: a live run with 2 questions per axis gave [−6.613, +6.113]. The report prints each interval as computed, and a claim depends only on which side of 0 it lies.

## Status of a claim

Every report is **confirmatory** or **exploratory**, printed on its second line. It is confirmatory when the run's stored experiment names a preregistration: a copy of `docs/preregistration.md`, filled in and committed before the run, with the hypotheses, the margin, the exclusions, and the runs that may pool with it. `run` refuses an experiment that names a file that is not there. A report of any other run is exploratory: its claims are what the current claim rules make of replies that were seen before those rules were fixed. Every run stored before version 2.9 is exploratory, and the claim rules did change after their replies were read: version 2.5 wrote the equivalence margin into the stored experiments by migration, 2.6 added the pair outcomes, and 2.8 changed how a truncated decline is read. A pooled report is confirmatory only when every run in it names a preregistration, and the preregistration must name the pool's rule; a pool formed after a run's report was read is a second look at the same answers, and its claims are exploratory whatever the single runs' status.

## When a claim is made

A model's **quadrant** names the pole on each axis whose side of 0 its interval clears. It is a sign with no magnitude: an interval of [+0.02, +0.08] names the positive pole as surely as one of [+0.6, +0.8], while a pair claim carries a margin; read the quadrant beside the position. It is withheld if any of these holds:

- an interval contains 0;
- an axis has fewer than 10 scored questions;
- the share of the model's trials on an axis that were answered is below `coverage_floor`;
- any of the model's trials is still pending.

An **ordering** of two models on one axis says which is higher, from the sign of the mean gap. It is withheld if any of these holds:

- the Holm-adjusted p is not below 1 − L;
- fewer than 10 questions were scored by both models;
- either model's answered share on the axis is below `coverage_floor`;
- any trial of the run is still pending, because Holm ties every pair's p-value to every other pair's.

An **equivalence** of two models on one axis says their gap lies within ±`equivalence_margin`. It is withheld under the ordering's question, answered-share, and pending rules, and when the equivalence test's Holm-adjusted p is not below 1 − L. An ordering and an equivalence can both be asserted: the gap is then real and smaller than the margin.

Every rule is checked, and a withheld claim lists every rule that failed. A withheld ordering never means the two models are equal; only an asserted equivalence says their gap is within the margin.

The two floors are conventions, not derived rules. The 10-question floor comes from the calibration below, whose floor scenarios cover within 0.01 of their target with 10 questions, the strong-questions one five standard errors below it; the detectable effect each interval prints is a sensitivity power analysis at 80% power, in the terms of Lakens (2022). The answered-share floor, `coverage_floor`, of 0.8 sits between the 5% missing below which a complete-case analysis is unremarkable and the 40% above which a trial's result is read as hypothesis-generating (Jakobsen et al., 2017). The 5% figure assumes the missing data are not concentrated in one group, and declines here concentrate on particular items (see "Missing data"), so the floor is a convention and not a guarantee; the bound under "Missing data" says what the unanswered trials could do to a position whatever the floor.

## Diagnostics

The report shows, for each model and axis, numbers that say where a position comes from. None of them gates a claim.

- **Poles.** The mean answer value over the statements toward each pole. A model with a position and no lean toward agreeing shows the position on both; a model that agrees with every statement shows −a toward one pole and +a toward the other, and a position of 0.
- **Lean to agree.** Half the difference between the two pole means, in score units: positive when the model leans toward agreeing with whichever statement it sees, negative when it leans toward disagreeing. Language models lean toward "no" more often than people do (Braun, 2025), and the pair average cancels a lean of either sign.
- **Pole correlation.** Across the questions both of whose statements were answered, Pearson's r between the agreement the negative-pole statement draws and the agreement the positive-pole statement draws. Content gives a negative r: the more a model agrees with one side, the less with the other. A lean toward agreeing gives a positive r. It is undefined below three questions or without spread.
- **Consistent questions.** Each fully answered question is counted once: consistent when its two statements draw agreement on opposite sides of the midpoint, at the midpoint when either statement's mean agreement sits exactly on it, and on the same side when both statements draw agreement or both draw disagreement. The three counts sum to the questions. The midpoint count is a measure of midpoint use, not of inconsistency: a model that answers the midpoint on one statement has taken no side to be consistent on. The consistent count and the pole correlation assume the two statements are contradictories, so that agreeing with one is disagreeing with the other; a pair that leaves a middle policy which rejects both puts a model holding it on the same side, and its position is unaffected, since rejecting both still scores 0.
- **Spread.** The position recomputed from each cell's answers alone, where a cell is one display order, numbering, and instruction wording; the lowest and highest cell positions are shown, over cells with at least two scored questions. With one repetition a cell holds one answer per phrasing, so cell positions are coarse. Pooled runs merge cells by wording.
- **Unstable questions.** Questions whose score changes sign between cells.
- **Use of the scale.** The share of the model's answered trials on the axis at each scale point, the midpoint share, the share at the two ends, and the standard deviation of the ratings. Language models use the ends of a scale far less than people do (Lukauskas & Šarkauskaitė, 2026), and the audit's "Ends" column reports the same share for its simulated ratings.
- **Split of the question scores.** How many question scores lie above 0, below it, and at it; the mean |score| beside |position|; the number of facets whose mean opposes the position's sign; and the facet-balanced position, the mean of the facet means, undefined while a facet of the axis has no scored question. A facet-balanced position far from the item-weighted one says the position leans on the facets with the most items. A position near 0 with a large mean |score| comes from stances that cancel across questions, a small one from answers near the midpoint. The split is printed because strongly partisan positions on single issues can offset each other in an aggregate score (Aldahoul et al., 2025); the reading of mean |score| against |position| is this tool's own. The report also names the three question scores farthest from the position: dropping one question can never move the position outside its own interval, and the farthest scores are the ones that pull it most.
- **Statement agreement.** Among statements answered two or more times, the share whose answers all lie on one side of the midpoint; and ICC(1) with statements as groups over the ratings, the share of rating variance that lies between statements rather than within them. A model that answers by content agrees with itself across the layouts and wordings; one that answers by layout does not. The report also counts the statements rated the same in every cell and those whose ratings span two or more points. At temperature 0 each cell holds one draw, so both counts are floors on how much the model's answers vary, not a measurement of it.
- **Unequal scale use.** For each pair and axis, the two models' rating standard deviations, and a warning when the larger is at least 1.5 times the smaller. The paired test compares means of a clamped scale: a model that uses the ends more is pulled toward 0 less than one that answers near the midpoint, so the gap between them, and an ordering asserted on it, is partly a difference of style rather than of stance (Liddell & Kruschke, 2018). The 1.5 ratio is a convention; the calibration's unequal-scale-use row gives the false-ordering rate at a ratio of 2. The warning gates nothing.
- **Sign stability.** For each pair and axis, the number of cells in which the two models' mean gap over the questions both scored there lies on the same side of 0 as the headline gap, over the cells where both scored two or more questions. A cell's gap rests on one answer per phrasing, so it tests nothing; the count says whether the ordering is the same way round under every layout and wording. It is undefined when the headline gap is not estimable or is exactly 0.
- **Between runs.** A pooled report gives, for each model asked in two or more runs, ICC(3,1) between its runs over the questions every run scored, per axis: 1 when the runs order the questions alike and differ by at most a shift, 0 when they agree no more than chance. It is read on Koo and Li's (2016) bands: below 0.5 poor, to 0.75 moderate, to 0.9 good, above excellent. A low value between runs weeks apart can mean an unstable model or a changed host; the report's host counts and run dates say which is possible. Between two runs that differ in their wording sets the ICC mixes retest agreement with the wordings' effect; only runs with the same wordings measure retest alone. The reading departs from Koo and Li in two ways. They apply the bands to the 95% confidence interval of the estimate, and the report reads the point estimate. They recommend the absolute-agreement form for test-retest, and ICC(3,1) is a consistency form, which a shift between runs does not lower. Their bands were set for human raters, so they are read here as conventions.
- **Position by wording and by layout.** The position recomputed from the answers at each wording, pooled over the layouts, and at each layout (one display order and numbering), pooled over the wordings; also at each display order and each numbering alone. Each needs two scored questions. The wording range and the layout range are the highest such position minus the lowest. The range says how far the wordings or the layouts move the position; it does not estimate one wording's effect, since each level is asked once.
- **Cell variance shares.** Over the full grid of wordings and layouts, the share of the cell positions' variance that each factor's main effect carries, as η², and the residual, which holds every interaction. It needs a position in every cell of the grid and spread between the cells; a factor at one level carries 0.

## Missing data

- A decline, an unreadable reply, a lost request, and a pending trial all count as unanswered. A hedged reply counts as answered: the number is read and the wording is only counted.
- **What is estimated when trials are unanswered.** The quantity is the model's mean question score at this endpoint over the trials it answered. This is a complete-case analysis: it assumes a declined or lost trial would have drawn an answer like the answers given. In the vocabulary of ICH E9(R1), a decline can be an intercurrent event, and asking what the score would be had the model not declined is that guideline's hypothetical strategy; a lost request is missing data, which the guideline does not count as an intercurrent event. Unanswered trials are not imputed and are not scored as the midpoint; they lower the answered share, a share below `coverage_floor` withholds the claim, and a question with an unanswered statement is not scored. Refusals concentrate on particular items and particular models (Kurtulus & Kruspe, 2025; Wightman, Bied & De Bie, AIES 2026), so the answers given can differ from the answers withheld, and the next two numbers say how much that could matter.
- **Unanswered per axis.** The report counts each model's unanswered trials on each axis, the declines among them toward each pole's statement, and the questions dropped because one statement drew no answer, with each one's unanswered count.
- **Bound.** The position recomputed with every unanswered trial filled at −1, and again at +1, dropped questions included. The position lies between the two under any answers to those trials (Horowitz & Manski, 2000); when every trial weighs the same, the bound is 2 × (1 − answered share) wide, so at the coverage floor it can span 0.4. Each gap carries the same bound, filling the second model low and the first high for its low end and the reverse for its high end. A claim both ends of the bound would still make holds under any answers to the unanswered trials; the bound gates nothing. The bound scores each dropped question at the fill, while the printed position drops it, so after a refusal of one whole statement the printed position can lie outside its own bound: the bound is read against the position with the dropped questions scored.
- **Flip.** For an interval that clears 0, the fill that pulls it to 0: over 41 fills from −1 to +1, in steps of 0.05, the fill at or below which an interval above 0 no longer lies above it, or at or above which one below 0 no longer lies below. It reads as "the interval crosses 0 if the unanswered trials averaged −0.55 or past it". A fill far out on the interval's own side can also end the claim, because it makes the filled questions outliers and widens the interval; the nearest such fill is reported apart, as "widens across 0 at +0.50 or past it". Each is undefined when no fill does so, when the interval contains 0 as it stands, or when nothing is unanswered. Gaps carry no flip, because Holm ties each pair's threshold to every other pair's.
- A request rejected by the provider is lost at once. A timeout, a connection failure, a rate limit, or a server error is retried up to `max_retries` times, and then lost.
- A bad key, an exhausted balance, or a model the provider does not serve stops the affected model or endpoint. Its trials stay pending until the run resumes.

## Calibration

`scripts/statistical_audit.py` simulates runs of two models and counts how often the intervals cover, how often an ordering or an equivalence is asserted, and how far the position sits from the stance. Each simulated rating is the scale's center plus the question's stance, a question effect, a statement slant and a repetition's noise, shown as the condition displays it, pulled by the scenario's lean toward the first-listed choice, clamped to the scale, and read back. In a three-wording scenario each wording also shifts every rating toward a pole: by one integer draw shared by both models, one per model, or one per question, with the standard deviation the scenario names, in scale points. Two scenarios draw no effects at all: one answers the midpoint every time, the other a uniform random point of the scale. The scenarios away from the null give both models a stance of one point (+0.5 in score units), give the second model twice the first's spread, add a point of agreement to the negative-pole statements alone, let the two models share 90% of the question effect, move 40% of the second model's questions one point (a true gap of 0.2), or fix the questions in seven facets at offsets of −1, 0, or +1.

Two targets are read. The **population position** is a model's expected question score, computed exactly from the scenario's integer effects and the clamping; the **stance** is what its points would read as without clamping or lean. The t-interval estimates the first; a reader wants the second; "Bias" is their difference, exact. "Coverage" counts the models, over the runs, whose two intervals cover their population position; "Stance coverage" the same against the stance. "Orderings" is the share of runs that assert an ordering: a false-positive rate when the two stances are equal, every row but one, and a power on the gap-0.2 row. "Equivalences" is the share that assert an equivalence: a power where the true gap is 0, a false-positive rate on the gap-0.2 row. "Ends" is the share of simulated ratings at either end of the scale. ± is the Monte Carlo standard error, computed with the run as the unit, so two models whose misses are correlated within a run do not count as independent draws; the file holds a 95% interval for each rate.

That commit predates the public history, which starts at a later version, so it cannot be checked out from the published repository; the file records it as the code that produced the numbers. The observed scenarios take their spreads from run 69e4b52438d6 of the retired 66-question revision of the bundled political questionnaire (hash 81a67b7c…, see `docs/questionnaire.md`, "Revision"): the medians over four models and both axes of the question effect's standard deviation (0.53 points), the statement slant's (0.35) and the noise around a phrasing's mean (0.58). In one wording, and in three wordings that shift nothing, they cover at their target: 0.951 and 0.948.

The floor scenarios cover within 0.01 of their target, from 0.942 to 0.952. Strong questions at the 10-question floor are the low end, 0.942, five standard errors below the target. The misses fall on both sides of the population position in near-equal numbers (566 below, 607 above), so the interval is too narrow rather than biased. Coverage is 0.946 at 15 questions and 0.944 at 20. The noisy scenario has 43% of its ratings at the scale's ends and covers at 0.948, so clamping alone does not explain the shortfall; the strong question effects leave few distinct question scores at the floor, where the t-interval's variance estimate is least stable. A lean of two points toward the first-listed choice is cancelled by the two presentation factors: that scenario covers at 0.952.

A wording shift that moves every question alike is not covered. The shifts are integer draws, so a standard deviation of 0.25 points leaves most wordings unshifted and moves one in about twenty by a whole point; that alone lowers coverage to 0.841. At 0.5 points coverage is 0.452, and at 1 point 0.248, while the false-ordering rate stays at its target because both models move together, and at 1 point the two models, moved alike, are declared equivalent in 1% of runs. A shift of 0.5 points that each model draws on its own leaves coverage at 0.442 and asserts a wrong ordering in 56% of runs. A shift each question draws on its own is absorbed into the spread between questions: coverage 0.951. So the interval covers the mean over the listed wordings, as the estimand says; it does not cover a wording outside the list, and the per-wording positions and the wording range in the report are the only measure of how far one would move it.

The two responders without effects are the floor a position is read against. A model that answers the midpoint every time scores 0 on every question, so its interval is the point 0, no ordering is ever asserted against another such model, and two of them are equivalent by construction. A model that answers at random covers at 0.949 and is wrongly ordered at 0.050, with 40% of its ratings at the scale's ends: its position is 0 with an interval of the width `plan` prints for the experiment, and nothing in the interval alone tells it from a model that holds the centre. Two random responders are declared equivalent in 80% of runs: their gap is 0 and the 24 draws behind each question score leave it little spread, so an asserted equivalence says two models' forced answers agree, not that either holds a view. The use-of-the-scale, split, and statement-agreement diagnostics do.

Away from the null, the interval keeps covering the population position, which is not the stance. A stance of +0.5 with the observed spreads has a population position of +0.472: clamping at the scale's end pulls it 0.028 toward 0, and the interval covers the stance in 92.5% of runs instead of 95%. With strong question effects the same stance reads as +0.300, a bias of −0.200, and the interval covers the stance in 52% of runs; a model whose ratings sit at the ends 56% of the time, as a stored model's do, is read a fifth of a score unit nearer the centre than it stands. Two models of equal stance that use the scale unequally are not ordered when both sit at 0, but at a stance of +0.5 the broader model reads as +0.362 against +0.472, and an ordering between them, which is one of style, is asserted in 24% of runs; the per-pair scale-use warning marks such a pair. A lean of one point toward agreeing with the negative-pole statements alone gives a model with no stance a population position of −0.236, which its interval covers in 95% of runs and which lies on the wrong side of 0: the interval is right about what the forced answers average to and says nothing about the stance, and no diagnostic separates the two. The equivalence test's power at a true gap of 0 rests on how alike the two models order the questions: two models sharing none of the question effect are never declared equivalent, two sharing 90% of it are in 74% of runs, and the same two with a true gap of 0.2 are ordered in 98% of runs and declared equivalent in 0.03%. Questions fixed in seven facets at offsets of a point cover at 0.998: the t-interval over questions carries the variance between facets that a fixed frame leaves out, as "What the interval covers" says, so it is conservative there, not wrong.

## Related work

- **Human-facing tests.** The Political Compass Test, 8values, SapplyValues, and the Political Coordinates Test are the two-axis tests this form comes from. 8values and SapplyValues, whose item files are public, ask each statement once, with no paired opposite, and print scores with no interval; the other two were not checked item by item. compass-bench takes their two-axis form and none of their items.
- **Nearest design.** Ceron et al. (TACL 2024) ask language models 239 voting-advice statements from seven European countries. Each statement comes with three paraphrases, a negation, and a semantic opposite. Each is asked under six prompt templates in both label orders, with 30 sampled responses per prompt. A stance is read only for a statement that passes every reliability test: a bootstrap interval on the sampled responses, the same stance under paraphrase and label order, and the reverse stance under negation and opposite.
- **What is shared and what differs.** compass-bench shares the opposite statement and the label-order variation. It asks on a five-point scale where Ceron et al. read a binary stance. It infers a position on an axis with a t-interval over questions, and compares models with Holm-adjusted tests and equivalence tests. It keeps every item in the mean, where Ceron et al. filter unreliable statements out first.
- **What compass-bench omits.**
  - *Statement paraphrases.* Each pole has one statement. The instruction wordings an experiment lists vary the request, not the statement.
  - *Answer distributions.* Each cell holds one reply at temperature 0, the modal answer, not a sample of answers.
  - *Open-ended elicitation.* Every reply is a forced number. Models answer differently in an open-ended setting (Röttger et al., ACL 2024).
  - *Refusal as an outcome.* A decline counts as unanswered and enters the bound under "Missing data"; it is not scored or modelled.
  - *Human or party anchors.* No person, party, or population has answered these items, so the scale has no external reference point.

## Limits

- **Calibration.** The audit simulates replies, and real replies can differ. When questions differ strongly at the 10-question floor, intervals cover less often than their level states: 0.942 against 0.95, 0.946 at 15 questions and 0.944 at 20. The interval covers the population position, the mean of the forced answers under the scale's clamping, and that position sits nearer 0 than the stance by 0.03 at a stance of +0.5 with the observed spreads and by 0.20 with strong question effects; a stored model with most of its ratings at the scale's ends is read nearer the centre than it stands. A shift shared by every question under a wording is not covered at all, and one a model draws on its own inflates wrong orderings; see the calibration. The observed scenarios take their spreads from a stored run; the audit does not resample stored replies themselves.
- **Construct validity.** That a questionnaire measures what its axes name rests on its author's claim. No factor analysis, reliability study, or item-bias study exists. `docs/questionnaire.md` records how the bundled axes are defined, which facet each item covers, and what an item-writing audit found: negations, statements with two claims, justifying clauses, unequal extremity between poles, and harm-coded wording. The per-facet scores in the report show where a position comes from; they are means over 5 questions in the political questionnaire and 2 to 12 in the others, and gate nothing.
- **Prompting.** A run asks every question with one prompt template, which an experiment can set. It asks with one instruction unless the experiment lists several as an `instruction` factor; then each wording's shift averages out over the listed wordings, but no wording outside the list. The bundled political-compass experiment lists three wordings, and `plan` warns when an experiment lists one. The three wordings ask three constructs, agreement with the statement, the model's view, and its opinion; the wording range the report prints is the evidence on how far they differ, and no design choice makes them equal. The report shows the position at each wording and layout, their ranges, and the share of the cell variance each factor carries; with three wordings the wording share rests on two degrees of freedom. The interval treats the wordings as fixed, so a shift shared by every question under a wording lowers its coverage, as the calibration shows; it does not estimate a wording's effect. Each question has one statement per pole, and every bundled questionnaire is in English.
- **Elicitation.** Every reply is forced into a number; the prompt offers no way to decline, and a decline counts as unanswered. Models asked the Political Compass Test without a forced choice mostly gave no valid answer, most often saying they could not express an opinion; how many answered at all depended on the forcing wording, and minimal paraphrases of the question moved their positions (Röttger et al., ACL 2024). So a position here is the model's answer under compulsion, not a view it volunteers. Asked who is asking when no system prompt names an asker, frontier models name an auditor, a researcher, or an academic. A stated conservative asker lowers the share of their answers closer to Democrats by 28 to 62 percentage points, and a stated progressive one changes little (Törnberg & Schimmel, 2026); the bundled runs measure the answer to that default asker. The prompt is a recognisable survey item: GPT-4, Claude 3, and Llama 3 identified a personality survey from five of its items with over 90% accuracy, and telling a model it is completing such a survey shifted its answers even on a single question (Salecha et al., 2024). Neither result has been shown on political items. Each statement is asked in its own request with no earlier turns, which removes order effects between items but not the model's recognition of the task, and the administration mode itself moves answers by model (Kreutner et al., 2025). The reply comes before any reasoning: a number alone. With `max_tokens` at 8 in the bundled experiment, a reply that starts to decline is cut off and read as a decline, so compulsion is partly enforced by truncation, and the unanswered trials are missing by construction rather than at random; each report counts the declines and format failures under "Outcomes"; across the five finished stored runs, 11,955 replies were answered, 1 was read as a decline, 1 as a format failure, and 11 were lost in transport. A run that asks for reasons first is a different elicitation (Libovický, 2026) and is not comparable with these positions; the bundled `political-compass-reason-first` experiment exists for that comparison (free-text models, one wording, 600 trials per model) and never pools with a direct run, since the prompt template is part of the pool key. The bundled `political-compass-awareness` experiment adds a fourth wording that names the task as a survey for a research audit, 600 trials per model more than the main experiment; the report's position per wording compares it with the three neutral ones. Neither arm has been run. A reasoning endpoint, whose hidden chain precedes the number, is a different configuration: `pool` refuses to combine it with a direct endpoint under one model id, and under two ids the report compares them as two models, with the elicitation label on their pair and the `plan` warning on the experiment. The bundled six-model experiment is such a case: four models answer at reasoning off within 8 tokens, and two, whose endpoints reject reasoning off, at reasoning low within 512 and 64 tokens, so the eight pairs across the two groups carry the label.
- **Reference population.** 0 is the scale's midpoint, not the centre of any population. No person has answered these items, so a position has no percentile and no human meaning; it is a point on this instrument's scale, comparable only with other configurations on the same questionnaire hash and scale form. Language-model surrogates predict the mean human answer to a survey item and explain little of how individuals deviate from it (Ahn et al., 2026), and digital twins built from individuals' data correlate weakly with those individuals' answers, r = 0.20 on average (Peng et al., 2026), so a human comparison would describe one number even if it existed.
- **No persona.** No run asks a model to answer as a person or a group. Prompting a model with a demographic identity flattens the group's range of answers (Wang et al., 2025), and matching individuals takes per-respondent data: agents built from interviews reached 83% of participants' own retest consistency on General Social Survey items, against 74% for agents built from demographics alone (Park et al., 2024). A persona prompt is also a system prompt, which makes a different configuration that never pools with the default. The bundled runs measure the unprompted default respondent on purpose: the respondent an API caller gets without a system prompt. A consumer product adds a system prompt of its own, which no bundled run sends. An experiment that wants a persona sets `system_prompt`, and the report prints it first.
- **Positions near 0.** A position near 0 can mean a model whose forced answers sit near the scale midpoint, one that answers 0 on every question, or one whose answers cancel across questions or across layouts. Other instruments report the third case, answers that cancel or collapse to the centre (Rozado, 2024; Debevc et al., 2026); the first two are this tool's own distinction, and their scales have no midpoint to answer 0 on. The consistency diagnostics and the per-facet scores separate these cases; the position alone does not. `plan` prints the half-width a responder answering at random on the scale would show at the experiment's question count, so a position's interval can be read against it before any money is spent: on the bundled 5-point scale with 4 layouts and 3 wordings it is about 0.06 on x, with 35 questions, and 0.05 on y, with 40.
- **Scale treatment.** Ratings are averaged as if the distances between scale points were equal. A metric model of ordinal ratings can invert the sign of an effect, and averaging several items does not remove the problem (Liddell & Kruschke, 2018). A pooled forced-choice report reads each model against the same model's native-scale pool, when one exists for the same questionnaire, settings, and system prompt: it prints the share of native answers at the midpoint per axis and, when that share reaches 0.5, labels the forced-choice position "forced, not held", since the midpoint would have absorbed most of the answers it rests on. The floor is a reading convention after Khan, Casper & Hadfield-Menell (2025), whose model chose "no preference" on every item of a 5-point scale and produced a full ranking once the midpoint was removed. The 5-point scale and its forced-choice form measure different things: removing the midpoint moved all but one of the RLHF-tuned models toward the weak agree and disagree points more than it moved the Llama 2 base models (Tjuatja et al., TACL 2024), and a model that would pick the midpoint on every question has no neutral answer left. Runs on the two scales never pool.
- **Language.** Every bundled questionnaire is in English. A model's answers to the same items shift with the language they are asked in (Helwe, Balalau & Ceolin, ACL 2025), and translating the questions does not reliably move the answers toward the opinions of that language's speakers (Durmus et al., 2023), so a translated questionnaire is a different instrument: it is its own file with its own hash, and its runs never pool with the English ones.
- **Instrument.** A questionnaire of abstract statements can disagree with a measure built from concrete decisions, such as how a model votes on real referenda (Barmettler, 2026). A quadrant here is a result on these statements asked this way; it makes no claim about what the model does in use. The y axis of the bundled political questionnaire measures the state's coercive reach into individual conduct. It is not the Political Compass Test's social scale or the GAL-TAN scale of cultural values, so a "libertarian" or "authoritarian" here does not carry over to those instruments; `docs/questionnaire.md`, "Axis definitions", names the items that score against the cultural coding.
- **Providers.** OpenRouter may route a model slug to different hosts and precisions. An endpoint can pin one host, refuse fallbacks, and filter by precision; `plan` warns when it does not, and the report counts each model's replies per host, per served model string, and per system fingerprint, with the time its first and last reply arrived, so a run that drew on several hosts or backends shows it. The precision served is not reported by OpenRouter, so only the filter sent is recorded; the system fingerprint, where a host reports one, is the only trace of the backend configuration. A hosted model changes under one name: models queried at pinned checkpoints showed step changes on dates with no known update (Cen et al., 2025), and versions 0613 and 1106 of GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4 differed by a statistically significant rightward shift on both axes of an adapted Political Compass Test, with regression coefficients of about 1 to 3 (Liu, Panwang & Gu, 2025), so `pool` warns when a model's runs name different served model strings or were created 28 days or more apart, runs under different system prompts never pool, and every report prints the date 28 days after a model's last reply as the date its position is due a re-check. A slug without a date token can be re-pointed under one name; a dated one names the weights, not the host behind them. A gateway can accept a sampling parameter the model's host does not apply: OpenRouter accepted `temperature` for OpenAI reasoning models that do not support it (Akimitsu, 2026). So `run` records what the host lists as supported and warns when a sent parameter is missing from it; `top_p` is sent only when set, and an unset `top_p` leaves the host's default in force, unrecorded. Endpoints of one open-weight model serve different distributions: 11 of 31 tested endpoints differed from the reference weights (Gao, Liang & Guestrin, ICLR 2025). `run` also records when the slug was listed and warns under 28 days; that warning rests on the assumption that hosts differ most soon after a model's release, which no cited study measures.
- **Layouts.** The four layouts and the listed wordings are fixed and asked in full; the interval does not treat them as a sample. Treating the layouts as random would widen the stored runs' intervals by about a third to a half on half the axes, and treating the wordings as random adds a variance component estimated on two degrees of freedom; both stay fixed, and the per-layout and per-wording positions are what the report says about them.
- **Reliability.** The runs hold one respondent per configuration, so the report computes no reliability coefficient, item-total correlation, or dimensionality test. Other work computes such coefficients for a language model by treating prompt variants as respondents: Serapio-García et al. (2023) build simulated respondents from persona descriptions and instruction variants and report Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω. compass-bench does not, because its cells are four layouts times the listed wordings, which are fixed presentations of one respondent and not a sample of respondents. A correlation between the x and y positions over a handful of models says nothing; the facet means and the question-level diagnostics are what the report has.
- **Reader.** The reader adds no error on a bare integer reply, which every stored reply was. Its decline and format-failure rules have fired on one live reply each across 11,955, so their agreement with a second reader is untested on real declines, and the structured form cannot decline at all: a schema that allows only a number leaves no way to say no.
- **Repetitions.** Temperature 0 does not make a hosted model deterministic: models at deterministic settings did not give repeatable outputs across runs (Atil et al., 2024). One named cause is that inference kernels are not batch-invariant, so the same request can answer differently under different server load (He et al., 2025). Short number-only replies are the most stable case. On these items the share of identical answers was measured once, from the ledger of runs 69e4b52438d6 and 64e08546e7df, whose standard wording asked the same 2,112 prompts 1h47m apart on 2026-11-01 at the same hosts: the same rating came back for 0.989 of claude-haiku-4.5's prompts, 0.943 of kimi-k2.6's, 0.871 of grok-4.3's, and 0.857 of deepseek-v4-flash's; the changes were one scale point in either direction about equally often, the question scores of the two runs correlated at ICC(3,1) 0.875 to 0.999, no position moved by more than 0.031, no ordering changed, and one quadrant did, whose interval end sat at −0.002. A pooled report line that recomputes these shares is a listed follow-up. A repetition at temperature 0 therefore says little about reliability; the presentations and wordings are the alternate forms (Löhn et al., INLG 2024), and more questions, not more repetitions, narrow an interval: in those runs the repeat noise contributed 0.001 to 0.009 to a position's standard error, the questions 0.02 to 0.06. A reasoning endpoint answers after a hidden chain the run does not vary; the report says how many of its completed replies carried reasoning tokens and how many replies were truncated at `max_tokens`, so an endpoint that asked for reasoning and got none, or ran out of room, is visible.
